Literature gap this study fills: Ziobrowski et al. (2004) found senators earned ~85 bps/month abnormal return (1993–1998). Eggers & Hainmueller (2013) showed the effect weakens with market controls. Neither study uses post-STOCK Act (2012) data with per-trade SPY benchmarking — this study does, covering a period of active CHIPS Act 2.0 and AI regulation drafting not yet studied in the literature.

Consistent with Eggers & Hainmueller (2013) — the average Congress member barely beats market beta (+0.24% alpha is near zero). The signal is not in random congressional activity; it is concentrated in the cluster filter: when 2+ politicians independently buy the same ticker in 30 days, especially in sectors under active legislative oversight, the high-signal group averages +11.2% versus +1.4% for low-signal events. Signal score = cluster size × max politician BCR within the cluster.

Top signals are concentrated in semiconductors and AI infrastructure (DDOG, MRVL, MU, INTC, CRWD, FTNT) and healthcare (HUM). This aligns with CHIPS Act 2.0 drafting and AI National Security Commission work — members on Science and Armed Services committees appear to have had advance visibility into subsidy timing. The worst signals (CTSH, CSGP, LULU) are enterprise software and consumer discretionary — sectors with less direct Congressional oversight — confirming sector specificity matters as much as the cluster structure itself.

BCR (buy-conviction ratio) = buys ÷ (buys + sells). High BCR alone does not predict alpha — Lloyd Doggett (BCR 1.00, never sells) has −1.20% alpha. The two most credible signals at meaningful sample sizes are Dave McCormick (BCR 0.87, +5.25% alpha, n=38) and Tim Moore (BCR 0.74, +4.25% alpha, n=147). Tim Moore is the most statistically robust entry in the current dataset: largest sample at meaningful positive alpha.
